About Three Cities

Three cities, three rhythms, nobody controlling the weather.

I made Three Cities, a generative music piece using the live weather in three places I’ve called home. I grew up and studied music in Boston, lived and performed in Copenhagen, and now live in London, where I produce and teach music and build instruments like this.

Each city runs its own Euclidean rhythm. Temperature sets the pattern length, clouds and wind shape what happens within it, and each city goes quiet after sunset. Depending on when you listen, you hear a different combination of cities and patterns.

The idea came from wanting to make something about these places without writing a conventional piece of music about them. Letting their weather play felt like an interesting way to keep them connected.

My training combined traditional music, production and studio engineering, followed by experimental music using Max/MSP at Harvestworks in New York. I love randomness and surprise in music as much as I love a beautifully crafted song. Sequencers, drum machines and modular synths give me that pleasure of setting something in motion and hearing it do something I didn’t expect. Here, the weather keeps moving the conditions around.

I also spent years making interactive and educational work and installations for the cultural sector, including at the Peabody Essex Museum. This brings those threads together: music, places, memory and a system that keeps changing.

You can replace my cities with your own and hear a different personal map. I’d be interested to hear how others approach weather-driven music and choose mappings that stay musically interesting over time. I’m also curious how you use Euclidean rhythms in your own compositions—whether as the starting point for a track, a way to build interlocking parts, or something you let evolve as you play.

Euclidean rhythm

Take a number of notes and spread them as evenly as possible across a number of steps. It is Euclid's algorithm, and it produces rhythms heard all over the world, like the Cuban tresillo (3 notes in 8 steps). Here, the weather picks both numbers.

Polymeter

The weather

Temperature in °C sets the steps (below zero, the city drops an octave). Humidity, wind, cloud or rain sets the hits. The sun's height opens each city's filter, and cloud cover adds reverb, so overcast cities sound foggier.
